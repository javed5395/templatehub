When two columns are safe

The blanket rule — never use two columns — exists because the failure is invisible. Your resume looks perfect on screen, the tracker scrambles it, and nobody ever tells you.

But the rule is broader than the risk. Parsers fail when they cannot decide the reading order of things that belong in sequence. Job title, employer, start date, end date — those must stay in one uninterrupted column, in order.

Everything else is a list where order barely matters. Contact details, software, certifications, languages. If a parser reads those in a strange sequence, nothing is lost, because each item means the same thing wherever it lands.

So the line is simple. Sequence in the main column. Lists in the panel. Follow that and a two-column resume is as safe as a one-column one.

When to use one column anyway

Even done correctly, a side panel is not always the right choice.

  • Government and public-sector applications. Older systems, stricter formats, and often a required template. Do not be clever.
  • Large-volume graduate schemes. Thousands of applications through one pipeline; the safest possible format wins.
  • When you have eleven years to fit. A panel costs roughly a third of your page width. Long histories need the space more than they need the styling.
  • When the advert names a file format or template. Follow the instruction exactly. It is sometimes a test of whether you read the advert.

And one that surprises people: if you are applying through a recruitment agency, the agency will frequently retype your resume into its own template before the client sees it. Elegant layout is discarded at that step; only the words survive.

What belongs in the panel

  • Safe: contact details, software and tools, certifications with years, languages, professional memberships, short skill lists, a portfolio link.
  • Never: job titles, employers, employment dates, bullets describing what you did, education dates, anything where order carries meaning.

A useful test before sending: copy the whole document, paste it into Notepad, and read the result. If job titles still sit next to their employers and their dates, you are fine. If the panel contents have been shuffled into the middle of your job history, move the panel contents until they cannot do damage.

Three rewrites for project work

Weak

Managed multiple construction projects from start to finish.

Strong

Deliver commercial builds of PKR 400M to 1.4B, managing 12 subcontractors on site.

Value range and subcontractor count answer the only two questions a construction client has.

Weak

Maintained a strong focus on health and safety at all times.

Strong

Zero lost-time injuries across 2.1M cumulative site hours.

Focus is an intention. Hours without injury is a record, and it is already in the site log.

Weak

Worked on various architectural projects of different sizes.

Strong

Lead designer on a 90,000 sq ft mixed-use retail development, concept through handover.

"Various" and "different sizes" are what people write when they have not checked the drawings for the actual area. Check them; the number is on the title block.
