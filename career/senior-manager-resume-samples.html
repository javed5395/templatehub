Set 11 · Executive · Resumes

Senior Manager, scope made plain.

The gap between manager and senior manager is not seniority, it is scope — how many people, how much budget, how many sites, how much of the P&L. Most resumes at this level bury all four and read like a slightly longer manager resume. These three state scope in the first four lines and spend the rest proving it moved.

Why this one works

Scope is the whole argument

The single most common reason a strong senior manager gets passed over is that the reader could not tell how big their job was. Two candidates can both write "managed the production team" — one had eleven people and one had four hundred and twenty.

State four dimensions of scope, in the profile, in the first two sentences:

  • Money. Revenue owned, budget controlled, output value, cost base managed.
  • People. Direct reports, total headcount, supervisors between you and the floor.
  • Footprint. Sites, shifts, regions, distributors, outlets, channels.
  • Accountability. Full P&L, cost centre only, or revenue without margin — say which.

The last one is quietly the most important. "Owned the P&L" and "managed the budget" sound similar and describe completely different jobs, and an experienced reader is trying to work out which one you had.

Every number needs a comparison

A number on its own invites the question "is that good?" and the reader has no way to answer it. Thirty-eight percent growth is extraordinary in a mature market and disappointing in a booming one. Ninety-four percent collection is excellent or average depending entirely on the sector.

So attach a reference point. The national average. The department benchmark. The prior year. The rest of the group. The target you were given.

This costs six words and it is the difference between a claim and evidence. It also signals something subtle and valuable: that you know your own numbers in context, which is exactly the habit a director is hiring for.

The people who left your team upward

One line separates a senior manager from a manager more reliably than any operational metric: how many people were promoted out of your team.

It is difficult to fake, expensive to achieve, and it tells a director you can be given a bigger organisation without becoming the bottleneck in it. Most resumes omit it entirely.

Count them. Territory officers who became area managers, supervisors who became managers, juniors who became seniors. Then write the number, and if you can, name what they became.

Three rewrites at senior level

Weak

Managed the production team and improved operational efficiency.

Strong

Run a three-shift operation producing PKR 4.8B annually with 420 staff and 11 supervisors.

The first tells a reader nothing about scale. The second answers every scale question at once.

Weak

Consistently exceeded sales targets across the assigned region.

Strong

Grew the region 38% over three years while national growth ran at 12%.

Exceeding target proves the target was set low or the market was kind. Outperforming the national rate proves it was you.

Weak

Responsible for improving customer satisfaction and reducing costs.

Strong

Cut cost per contact 31% by moving 40% of volume to self-service without NPS loss.

Both halves of the trade-off, the mechanism that achieved it, and the proof nothing broke.
