Why this one works
  • The glyphs are not decoration. A contact block is scanned, not read. A small mail shape is found faster than the word “Email”, and it costs no line of text.
  • The rail makes chronology visible. Dates sit above each role with a dot on a line, so a reader sees the shape of the career before reading a word of it.
  • A hairline instead of a filled panel. Two columns are separated without spending a solid block of ink, which keeps the sheet light and prints cleanly on a cheap printer.
  • Sans throughout, and a different accent. A serif reads as editorial. A designer applying for design work is judged on this choice before a single bullet is read.
  • The portrait is optional, and this is the honest position on it. A photo is expected across much of Asia, the Gulf and continental Europe, and unusual in the US, UK and Canada, where recruiters are often told to set those applications aside. Use it where it is normal; delete it where it is not. The slot takes one click either way.

The resume is not the portfolio

Creative applicants often spend their effort making the resume itself a design piece — unusual grids, custom type, colour blocks. It is understandable and it is usually a mistake, because the document is being judged on a different axis than the work.

The portfolio answers "can they design?". The resume answers everything a portfolio cannot: did this work achieve anything commercially, was it delivered on time, how much did it cost, how many people were managed, and can this person be left in a room with a client.

A creative director will forgive a plain resume from someone whose portfolio is strong. They will not forgive a beautiful resume that never explains whether the work sold anything.

The numbers creative work has

  • Commercial outcome. Conversion lift, drop-off reduction, sales change after a rebrand, click-through on a campaign.
  • Volume. Assets per month, commissions per year, videos produced, pages designed.
  • Speed. Turnaround time, production cycle before and after, delivery guarantees.
  • Audience. Followers gained, watch-through rate, subscribers, portfolio views.
  • Money. Budgets managed, client billings, cost saved through systems or templates.
  • Clients. Accounts held, repeat rate, retention, named brands where permitted.

Watch-through, repeat rate and turnaround are the three most persuasive and the three most commonly missing. All of them sit in an analytics tab or an invoicing folder you already have.

Making freelance look stable

Freelance experience triggers one specific worry in an employer: that you have never had to meet somebody else's standard, on somebody else's schedule, repeatedly. Everything in the freelance section should address that.

  • Give it a title and dates like a job. "Commercial Photographer, Self-employed, 2018 — Present" is a seven-year tenure. A scatter of individual projects is not.
  • Lead with retention. Repeat clients prove other people's standards were met more than once.
  • State volume annually. It shows a sustained workload rather than occasional jobs.
  • Name the business functions. Quoting, contracts, invoicing — proof you have run something, not just made things.
  • Keep any employed role, however old. It answers the doubt directly.

Three rewrites for creatives

Weak

Designed eye-catching packaging for a range of consumer products.

Strong

Rebuilt a packaging range that lifted in-store conversion 24% across 1,200 outlets.

"Eye-catching" is a judgement about your own work. Conversion is a judgement the market made.

Weak

Created engaging social media content for various clients.

Strong

Grew a brand TikTok account from 4,000 to 210,000 followers in 14 months.

Engaging is the intention. The follower trajectory is the evidence that it worked.

Weak

Freelance photographer working with a variety of clients since 2018.

Strong

Deliver 120+ commissions annually for a client base of forty, 70% of whom book again.

The first sentence could describe someone with four jobs a year. The second is a business.

Let the work speak, and make the resume open the door.
