Set 03 · Portfolio · Freelancer

Freelancer showcase, priced openly.

A freelance portfolio is not a job application. It is a sales document sent cold to someone who has never heard of you, and it has to answer three questions before they stop reading: what do you do, has it worked, and what does it cost. Three complete showcases — a writer, a developer, and a video editor — each with rates on the page.

Sample OneCold-send showcase · Rates included

Written to be attached to a first email to someone who has never heard of her. Every section below is one page or block of that document.

Sana Iqbal
Freelance content writer — B2B SaaS and fintech · Karachi
1 — The one line12 words
I write long-form content for B2B SaaS companies that need to rank. SaaS and fintech · 60+ published pieces · Working with 4 retained clients
A niche, not a service. "Freelance writer" competes with everyone; "long-form B2B SaaS content that ranks" competes with about forty people and charges three times as much.
2 — What I actually doFour lines
Long-form articles, 1,500–3,000 words, researched from primary sources and interviews Content briefs and keyword-mapped outlines for in-house teams Case studies, from customer interview through to approved copy Email sequences for onboarding and activation I do not write: press releases, social captions, or AI-assisted bulk content.
The "I do not" line is the most useful in the document. It filters out the work you would resent, and it signals a standard.
3 — Selected workThree, with results
Payment infrastructure company — 14-article series on cross-border settlement. Client-reported: organic traffic to the resource hub up 3.4× over nine months; two articles reached position one for their target term. HR platform — customer case study programme, 9 interviews to publication. Used by their sales team as the primary deck insert; the format is now their standard. Fintech startup — onboarding email sequence, 6 emails. Activation within 14 days up from 31% to 44% against the previous sequence.
Three pieces, each with an outcome and an honest source. A list of logos with no numbers is the default and it persuades nobody.
4 — How I workThe process, briefly
Brief and scope agreed in writing before anything starts One interview or research call per piece where the subject needs it First draft within 7 working days Two rounds of revision included; further rounds billed hourly You own the copy on final payment
Every line here prevents a specific argument later. Revision rounds and ownership are the two that cause most freelance disputes.
5 — RatesOn the page
Long-form article, 1,500–3,000 words — PKR 45,000–70,000 depending on research depth Case study, interview to approved copy — PKR 55,000 Email sequence, per email — PKR 12,000 Retainer, 4 articles per month — PKR 200,000, invoiced monthly 50% on commission for new clients. Net 14. Rush work inside 5 days, +30%.
Actual numbers. Ranges are fine; "contact for pricing" is not, because it costs the client an email to find out you are out of budget and most will not send it.
6 — What I need from youFour things
A named point of contact who can approve copy Access to a subject expert for one call per piece Your existing style guide, if one exists Feedback consolidated into one response per round Projects stall on approval, not on writing. These four remove most of it.
Stating requirements reads as professional, not demanding, and it sets expectations before the first invoice rather than during the third argument.
7 — How to startOne action
Reply with the topic and roughly when you need it. I will send a scope and a fixed price within two working days. sana.iqbal@email.com · +92 302 7741 663 · sanaiqbal.work
One instruction, one promise, and a timeframe. "Let me know if you are interested" ends a sales document with nothing to do.
Why this one works
  • The niche is on line one. B2B SaaS long-form. Narrowing is what allows the rate at the bottom of the page to be defended.
  • It says what she does not do. Three exclusions. It reads as a standard rather than a limitation, and it saves both sides a bad project.
  • The rates are real numbers. Ranges, deposit, payment terms and a rush premium. It disqualifies the wrong client before either party spends an hour.
  • The work has sourced outcomes. Client-reported traffic, a position, an activation percentage. Logos alone do not sell.
  • It ends with one action and a promise. Reply with a topic; get a scope in two days. Cold documents fail at the last line more often than anywhere else.

Technical freelancing sells differently. The buyer is usually assessing risk rather than craft, so the showcase leads with reliability and stack, and prices by day.

A day rate with a minimum, an emergency rate, and a retainer. Three ways to buy means three different budgets can say yes.

Repository access and a running local environment, or two hours with someone who has one One technical decision maker who can answer within a day Access to staging and to logs A clear definition of done for each piece of work Most overruns I have seen were caused by waiting for access, not by the work.
Blaming a delay on the client afterwards is a fight; naming the dependency beforehand is project management.
7 — How to start

Why this one works
  • Retention is the headline evidence. Three clients since 2023. For technical work, continued payment beats any stack list.
  • Paid discovery is built into the process. It ends unpaid scoping and gives cautious buyers a low-risk entry point.
  • Three ways to buy. Day rate, retainer, emergency support. Different budgets and different urgencies can all say yes.
  • One case pays for itself. AWS bill halved, fee recovered in month one. That is the case study a finance-conscious buyer forwards.
  • He implies he says no. "Whether it is something I should take on" changes the power balance of the entire document.

  • Volume and speed lead. 400 videos, 48 hours. For production work this is the pitch, and showreels are the commodity.

It is a sales document

A freelance showcase and a job portfolio look similar and do different jobs. A portfolio sent to a hiring manager is read by someone who already decided to hire; a showcase sent cold is read by someone who did not ask, is not sure they have a problem, and will stop after fifteen seconds if nothing lands.

That changes the structure. The niche goes first, the outcomes go second, the price goes on the page, and the last line tells them exactly what to do. Everything else is optional.

It also changes the tone. A freelancer who reads as an equal — someone with a process, a rate, requirements of their own, and other clients — gets treated as a supplier. A freelancer who reads as available gets treated as cheap labour, and the difference is entirely in the writing.

Why put rates on the page

The usual advice is to withhold price until you have had a conversation, on the theory that you can anchor higher once they want you. For cold-sent showcases this is wrong more often than it is right.

  • It disqualifies fast. A client whose budget is a fifth of your rate finds out in ten seconds instead of after two calls and a proposal.
  • It signals a settled business. People who quote per project after inspecting the client are assumed, correctly, to be pricing by what they think they can get.
  • It removes the cheapest negotiation tactic, which is asking you to name a number first in a context where you have no information.
  • It makes upsells visible. A retainer line on the page gets asked about; a retainer you planned to mention in the third call usually never comes up.

Ranges are fine, and so is "from". What does not work is "contact for pricing", because it asks the reader to spend effort to discover they cannot afford you, and most of them simply will not send the email.

Alongside the rate, always state: deposit, payment terms, what a revision round means, how many are included, and what rush work costs. Nearly every freelance dispute is one of those five, and each one costs a sentence to prevent.

Sending it cold

The showcase does not work on its own. It works attached to a short email that gives one specific reason for writing to that particular person.

  • Name the observation. "You published eleven videos last quarter and none were cut into shorts." One line of evidence you actually looked.
  • Say what you do, in one sentence, matching the observation.
  • Attach the showcase as a PDF under 10 MB, or link a page that opens without a signup.
  • Make the ask small. A reply, a sample, a fifteen-minute call — not a meeting to discuss a partnership.
  • Follow up once, after a week, and then stop. A second follow-up converts occasionally; a third damages the name you are trying to build.

On free samples: bounded ones work and unbounded ones do not. A sixty-second cut of a video they already published, a rewritten opening paragraph, a single-page audit — an hour of your time, clearly finite, demonstrably yours. A full spec project, a free trial week, or three concepts before payment is not a sample, and clients who ask for those tend to keep asking.

And keep the document alive. Update the numbers each quarter — pieces delivered, clients retained, the newest result. A showcase whose most recent work is two years old says something about your current pipeline that you did not intend to say.

Niche first, outcome second, price on the page.
