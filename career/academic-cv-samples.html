The academic CV, publications first.

An academic CV is not a long resume. It is a different document with a different reader, a different order, and no page limit — and the ordering is where most applications quietly fail. A search committee reads publications, funding and teaching, in that order, and stops when it has decided. Three samples: postdoctoral, assistant professor, and a full professor going for a chair.

Why this one works
  • Publications come before everything except the profile. On an academic CV this is the section that decides. Putting employment history first is the commonest structural error.
  • First authorship is marked. "[first author]" in bold. Committees read author position closely and should not have to count names.
  • Full citations, no abbreviations. Journal, volume, pages, year. A committee may look up any of them, and a truncated citation reads as evasive.
  • The grant states the role and the amount. Co-Investigator, PKR 2.4M, and which workstream. Vague funding claims are checked and are the fastest route to distrust.
  • Thesis title is a bullet, not a heading. It tells a reader the field in one line without consuming a section.

Why this one works
  • Funding is separated by role. "Principal Investigator" as the section heading. PI money and co-investigator money are weighed completely differently and must never be mixed.
  • Publications are marked as selected. "Selected Publications (19 total)" with a pointer to the full list. This is standard practice once the list is long and it respects the reader.
  • Teaching lists course codes and enrolment. A committee assessing teaching load needs the numbers; "taught various courses" is worthless to them.
  • Evaluation score is included. 4.6 out of 5 over six years is evidence. Claiming to be a dedicated teacher is not.
  • Refereeing appears under service. Journal review work signals standing in the field and is invisible anywhere else on the document.

Why this one works
  • The record-at-a-glance block does the shortlisting. Publications, h-index, citations, funding, supervision. A chair committee needs those five figures and should not have to count anything.
  • Doctoral completions have a follow-on statistic. "11 hold academic posts" measures the quality of supervision, not the quantity, and it is the strongest supervision claim available.
  • Funding lists the funder, not just the amount. Gates, Wellcome and WHO carry weight of their own, independent of the sums.
  • Only three publications are shown. At 84, listing all of them on the first pages buries everything else. Show the strongest three and supply the full list separately.
  • International service is a section. For a chair appointment, WHO advisory work and editorial board membership are precisely the standing being purchased.

A CV is not a long resume

The two documents share a name in casual speech and almost nothing else. A resume argues that you can do a job. An academic CV documents a record of scholarship, and is read by people who will verify parts of it.

  • Length. A resume is one or two pages. A CV is as long as your record — four pages early, twenty later. Nobody is impressed by brevity here.
  • Order. A resume leads with recent employment. A CV leads with publications and funding, because that is what is being assessed.
  • Selection. A resume omits ruthlessly. A CV is comprehensive, though very long lists may be marked "selected" with the full version supplied separately.
  • Tone. A resume persuades. A CV states. Adjectives about your own excellence read as unserious to a search committee.

Sending a resume for an academic post signals you do not know the field. Sending a CV to industry signals the same thing in reverse.

The order that matters

A search committee reads in a fixed sequence, and your document should match it. For a research-focused post:

  • Name, position sought, contact, ORCID
  • Short profile — three sentences with your output, funding and field
  • Education — degrees, institutions, years
  • Academic appointments
  • Publications — the section that decides
  • Research funding — separated by PI and co-investigator
  • Supervision — doctoral and masters completions
  • Teaching — courses with codes and enrolments
  • Service, review and professional bodies
  • Conferences and invited talks

For a teaching-focused post, move teaching above publications. That single change matters more than any amount of rewriting, and most applicants never make it.

Listing publications properly

  • Reverse chronological, newest first.
  • Full citations. All authors, year, title, journal, volume, pages. Never abbreviate to save space.
  • Bold your own name in every entry so position is visible instantly.
  • Mark first and corresponding authorship explicitly.
  • Separate the categories. Peer-reviewed articles, book chapters, conference proceedings, preprints and under-review work must not be mixed — mixing them is read as inflating the count.
  • Include DOIs where the submission format allows.
  • Never list an accepted paper as published, or a submitted one as under review at a journal that has not acknowledged it. These are checked.

If the list runs past about fifteen, mark the section "Selected Publications (n total)" and offer the complete list. This reads as confident, not evasive — provided the number is stated.

Funding is read closely

After publications, awarded funding is the most scrutinised part of an academic CV, because it is externally verified and hard to overstate without being caught.

State four things for every award: the funder, your role, the amount, and the period. Separate Principal Investigator awards from co-investigator ones under different headings — a committee that has to work out which is which will assume the less favourable reading.

Include unsuccessful applications only if the scheme is prestigious and you reached the final stage, and only in a clearly labelled subsection. Some committees value it as evidence of ambition; others read it as padding. If in doubt, leave it out.

Order it the way it is read.
