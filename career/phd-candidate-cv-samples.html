PhD candidate CV, mid-programme.

The hardest academic CV to write is the one where the work is genuinely unfinished. Papers under review, a thesis two years from submission, teaching that is real but unofficial. The instinct is to inflate or to apologise, and both cost you. Three samples: applying for a PhD, mid-programme applying for a fellowship, and writing up while applying for jobs.

Why this one works
  • Thesis status is in the job title line and repeated twice. Submitted March, viva expected June, available July. An employer planning a start date needs all three and should not have to ask.
  • "Dr. (cand.)" is used carefully. Not "Dr." — the degree is not awarded. This convention is understood in academia and using the full title early is noticed and held against you.
  • Industrial adoption is stated. "Adopted into the company pilot line" is the strongest possible evidence for an industrial R&D application.
  • Three first-author papers are marked. For a postdoctoral application this is the number that decides, and it should never require counting names.

Writing about unfinished work

A doctoral CV is mostly a description of things not yet complete, and there are only two ways to get it wrong: overstate and be caught, or understate and be passed over. The route between them is precision.

  • Published means it has appeared or is in press with a DOI. Nothing else.
  • Accepted means the journal has formally accepted it. Say which journal.
  • Under review means submitted and acknowledged. Name the journal and the submission month.
  • In preparation is worth listing only for one or two manuscripts genuinely close to submission, and label it plainly.

Never let an under-review paper sit unmarked in a published list. Reviewers check, checking is trivial, and being caught converts a strong application into a dishonest one in one line.

Show momentum, not volume

A third-year candidate cannot compete on publication count with a postdoc, and should not try. What a fellowship panel or hiring supervisor is judging is rate — whether output is arriving steadily and will continue after they fund you.

So show the trajectory. Papers with years attached, so the pattern is visible. Conference presentations across successive years. A submission pipeline. Software with growing usage. A thesis with a stated expected date.

Two papers in your second and third years reads better than two papers with no dates, because the first tells a panel what next year will probably look like.

What else counts

Doctoral candidates routinely omit half of what they have done, because it did not feel like part of the thesis.

  • Teaching and demarcation work. Labs run, students taught, materials rewritten.
  • Supervision. MS students you trained. It is real supervision and it counts.
  • Software, datasets and protocols you published, with usage figures.
  • Co-reviewing manuscripts with your supervisor — label the caveat honestly.
  • Organising reading groups, seminar series, conference sessions.
  • Industrial or clinical collaboration, and whether anything you produced was adopted.
  • Visiting research periods, with the funder and what came out of them.

For applications outside academia these matter more than the thesis itself, because they are the evidence that you can work with people, ship something usable and finish what you start.

Be precise about stage.
