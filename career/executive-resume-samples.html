Why this one works
  • Investor return is the headline KPI. For private mid-market, 4.4× to the investor is the number the reader cares about most, and it belongs in the strip.

  • Break-even period is included. Eight months versus fourteen is the single most useful number in multi-site retail, and it is almost never on a resume.

The four numbers at the top

Executive search moves fast and cruelly. A retained headhunter with sixty resumes is not reading for nuance — they are sorting by scale, then by relevance, then by evidence. The number strip at the top of these samples exists to serve exactly that sequence.

Choose four, and choose them from different families so they cannot be dismissed together:

  • Size. Revenue owned, budget, assets under management. Answers "is this our scale?"
  • People. Headcount led, direct reports, sites. Answers "can they run something this big?"
  • Growth or efficiency. EBITDA multiple, margin points, cost removed.
  • Return. Shareholder return, investor multiple, valuation change. Answers "did the owners do well out of it?"

Four is the right count. Three feels thin at this level; six stops being a summary and becomes another paragraph to read.

Growth needs a counterweight

The most common failure in senior resumes is a page of pure expansion. Revenue tripled, headcount doubled, markets entered, acquisitions closed. Read alone, it raises a question the candidate never answers: at what cost, and was any of it durable?

Experienced readers pair every growth claim with a control claim, in the same sentence where possible. Revenue grew and margin held. Expansion funded and gearing fell. Cost removed and no redundancies. Stores opened and break-even shortened.

This is not modesty. It is the difference between someone who grew a business and someone who inflated one, and the reader is looking for that difference on every line.

Board work is a separate currency

Non-executive directorships, advisory councils, audit committee seats and trustee roles are not jobs and should never be listed among them. Mixing them into the career history makes the timeline look chaotic and, worse, makes the reader think you held two full roles at once.

They belong in their own block, usually in the sidebar, with the organisation, the role and the year you joined. Three lines, no bullets, no explanation.

They matter more than people expect. A chairman recruiting a chief executive is also assessing whether you can sit on the other side of the table — and a candidate who already does is a materially different proposition.

One page, or two

The one-page rule is real for most of a career and stops being real somewhere around fifteen years and director level. Nobody expects a chief executive with a twenty-two-year record to compress it into a single sheet, and the attempt reads as inexperience.

Two pages is the ceiling. Three signals that you cannot distinguish what matters — which is the exact quality being hired for.

The discipline for page two: it holds older roles, education, governance and publications. It holds nothing a reader needs in order to decide. If your best argument is on page two, the page order is wrong, not the length.
