You have more than you think

A graduate resume looks empty because the writer applied a rule nobody actually holds: that only paid employment counts. Employers hiring graduates know you have not been employed. They are looking for evidence you can be given work and will complete it.

All of this counts, and all of it can be written in the same shape as a job:

  • Final year project. A brief, a team, a budget, a deadline, a result.
  • Internships, however short. Six weeks is six weeks.
  • Society and committee roles. Treasurer, president, event lead — these involve money, people and deadlines.
  • Tuition and freelance work. Self-employment with clients and outcomes.
  • Clinical rotations, lab work, teaching practice, field work. Supervised, but real.
  • Competitions, hackathons, case challenges, sports leadership.

Write each with a title, an organisation, dates and bullets. Same structure as employment. It is honest — the titles say what they were — and it fills a page with things that actually happened.

Where a graduate finds numbers

Everything above becomes twice as persuasive with a figure attached, and the figures exist.

  • Hours. Rotation hours, internship weeks, tuition hours per week.
  • People. Team size, students taught, committee members, event attendees.
  • Money. Project budget, society budget, sponsorship raised, fees earned.
  • Volume. Patients clerked, incidents logged, samples run, surveys collected.
  • Outcome. Grade achieved, percentage improvement, ranking, whether it was selected or published.

Check your logbook, your project report and your society accounts before you write. Ten minutes there is worth more than an hour of adjectives.

Four mistakes almost every graduate makes

Weak

Objective: To secure a challenging position in a reputable organisation where I can utilise my skills and grow professionally.

Strong

Mechanical engineering graduate with a first-class degree and a final year project that cut test-rig cycle time 34%. Seeking a graduate trainee engineer position.

One. The objective statement is about what you want. Replace it with what you have and what post you are applying for.

Weak

Final Year Project: Automated Test Rig

Strong

Designed and built an automated test rig for gearbox fatigue testing on a PKR 180,000 budget; cut test cycle time 34% against the existing manual rig.

Two. A project title is not a project. What was it, what did it cost, what did it achieve.

Weak

Skills: MS Office, Communication, Hard Working, Team Player, Fast Learner

Strong

Skills: SolidWorks, AutoCAD, ANSYS (basic), Preventive Maintenance, Technical Drawing

Three. The first list appears on every graduate resume in the country. The second is made of things that can be tested.

Weak

Pursuing ACCA. Currently studying.

Strong

ACCA — Applied Knowledge completed, 2025. Applied Skills in progress.

Four. Say exactly what stage you have reached. Vagueness about qualifications is read as a hidden problem, and it usually is not one.
