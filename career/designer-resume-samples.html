Why designers should send plain resumes

It feels wrong. You design for a living, the resume is a design object, and leaving it plain seems like a missed opportunity. Three reasons it is not.

The portfolio already carries the aesthetic argument, and carries it better — full-size, in context, with the reasoning attached. Nothing a resume can do competes with that.

A heavily designed resume competes with itself. The reader is trying to find out whether you have shipped anything measurable, and every decorative element is friction between them and that answer.

And restraint is itself legible to this audience. A designer who can leave space, hold a grid and resist ornament is demonstrating judgement on the page. To a design director, a disciplined plain resume reads as confidence; an elaborate one frequently reads as compensation.

Design outcomes, not descriptions

The commonest failure in design resumes is describing what was made instead of what it did.

  • Interfaces: completion rate, drop-off, task success, time on task, support tickets reduced.
  • Systems: components shipped, teams adopting, handoff time, design debt removed.
  • Research: sessions run, sample size, studies reused, decisions changed.
  • Physical and spatial: area delivered, budget variance, programme adherence, sites opened.
  • Brand and print: conversion, recall, production cost, rollout scale.

If a number genuinely does not exist — early-stage work, killed projects, confidential clients — use scale instead. Users affected, team size, project value, duration. Scale is always available and always better than an adjective.

The typographic rules of this set

For anyone rebuilding this by hand rather than downloading it, the discipline is small and specific.

  • One typeface, two or three weights. Mixing families is the fastest way to look unsure.
  • Three type sizes maximum in the body: name, section heading, text. Job titles differ by weight, not size.
  • One accent colour, used for no more than two things — section rules and employer names, for instance.
  • Left-aligned everything. Justified text creates rivers; centred text destroys the scan path.
  • Consistent spacing above headings, larger than the space below them, so sections group visually.
  • Generous margins. The instinct to fill the page is the instinct to be ignored.

None of this is decoration. It is what makes the page readable in the eleven seconds it will actually receive.

Let the portfolio carry it, and keep the page quiet.
