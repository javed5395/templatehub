Search for a merchant of record website template and you get almost nothing — either generic SaaS templates with a payments colour scheme dropped on top, or bespoke agency sites priced well beyond what a growing platform can justify. Almost none of them are built around what a modern MoR platform actually has to communicate: that it handles tax, compliance, liability and global currency so the seller does not have to. This is a guide to what a fintech payments platform website needs to get right, and what to check before you buy one.

What a Merchant of Record Website Is Really For

The biggest communication challenge for a merchant of record is that most potential clients have never heard the term before. They know they have a problem — selling across borders, dealing with VAT, handling chargebacks — but they do not yet know that an MoR is the name of the solution. The website has to do two things at once: name the problem in the visitor's words, and then introduce the solution in terms they can act on.

That means the first screen cannot open with "The Premier Global MoR Platform." It has to open with something like: "Sell in 190 countries. We handle the tax." Then, and only then, do you explain what a merchant of record is and why it matters.

A fintech platform website has one job before all others: remove the fear of complexity. Global payments, VAT registration in every country, currency risk, chargebacks — the prospect is already anxious. The site's entire job is to make the answer feel simple, safe, and handled.

The Eighteen Pages a Modern MoR Platform Website Needs

Most SaaS templates give you a homepage, a pricing page and a contact form. A serious merchant of record platform needs a full information architecture that addresses every buyer — the founder, the CFO, the developer and the legal team. In practice it comes to eighteen pages. This is the exact set we build to:

  • 01 · Landing — the outcome stated plainly. Sell globally, keep the complexity ours.
  • 02 · How It Works — the three-step explanation a non-lawyer can follow in under a minute.
  • 03 · Pricing — percentage fee, fixed component, payout frequency, conversion spread. No hidden costs.
  • 04 · Supported Countries — the full map of coverage with tax types handled per jurisdiction.
  • 05 · Tax & Compliance — VAT, GST, sales tax, digital services tax — every type filed, every jurisdiction listed.
  • 06 · Integrations — Stripe, Paddle, WooCommerce, Shopify, API — how the platform connects to what the client already uses.
  • 07 · Developer Hub — API overview, supported languages, average integration time, link to full documentation.
  • 08 · Security — PCI DSS level, SOC 2 type, ISO 27001, uptime SLA.
  • 09 · Payout & Currency — payout schedules, 47+ currencies, FX spread, local bank support.
  • 10 · Fraud & Disputes — chargeback handling, fraud scoring, dispute resolution timeline.
  • 11 · Enterprise — custom pricing, dedicated account management, volume SLAs.
  • 12 · Case Studies — named clients, real revenue figures, geographic expansion story.
  • 13 · Partners — referral programme, agency partners, technology partners.
  • 14 · Analytics Dashboard — the live view a client sees: revenue, tax collected, payout schedule.
  • 15 · About — founding story, leadership, regulatory licences held.
  • 16 · Legal Centre — terms of service, privacy policy, data processing agreement, subprocessor list.
  • 17 · Blog or Resources — the content that earns organic search traffic on "global VAT", "MoR vs PSP", "digital services tax".
  • 18 · Contact & Onboarding — a form that qualifies the lead and sets a call within one business day.

Eighteen pages only work if they are navigable. The top menu stays clean — How It Works, Pricing, Integrations, Security, and a Get Started CTA — with the country coverage, compliance detail, enterprise options and legal centre accessible from the footer and from contextual links within the page text.

Explaining Complex Payments Without Losing the Reader

The merchant of record model is not complicated, but it is unfamiliar. The mistake most MoR platforms make on their websites is to explain the mechanism before they have established the problem. A CFO reading your pricing page does not need to know what a digital services tax is — they need to know that you handle it so they do not have to file a return in Germany, France and Australia.

Three things make a payments platform website readable to a non-technical buyer:

  • Lead with outcomes, not features. "Sell in 190 countries" before "our tax engine handles 47 jurisdictions." The second sentence explains the first; it does not replace it.
  • Use steps, not paragraphs, for the How It Works page. A buyer who understands the flow in four steps will ask better questions on a sales call and close faster than one who read three paragraphs and is still unsure who signs what.
  • Put the scary number first. "We handle $2.4 billion in transactions annually" is a trust signal. Burying it in a case study, where only one in twenty visitors will find it, is a missed opportunity on every other page.

What the Pricing Page Has to Show

Pricing is where most MoR platforms lose enterprise prospects. A buyer who cannot find a clear fee structure assumes the price is negotiated downward from a high number, and they start the conversation defensive. A transparent pricing page — even one that says "enterprise pricing on request" for the largest tier — signals that the platform is confident in its value.

Every pricing page on a merchant of record website should state: the percentage per transaction, the fixed component per sale if any, the payout frequency (daily, weekly, monthly), the FX spread on currency conversion, and a plain statement of what the MoR service includes — tax filing, chargeback liability, fraud coverage. If any of those items are "contact us," say so explicitly rather than leaving the table blank.

Hidden costs — undisclosed FX spreads, surprise payout fees, late filing penalties that are passed through — are the single largest driver of chargeback escalations and contract non-renewals in the MoR category. Show everything. A competitor who charges less but buries the difference in a footnote will not keep the client past the first billing cycle.

The Security and Compliance Page No One Reads Until It Matters

Enterprise buyers skip the security page until the procurement team sends a questionnaire. Then it becomes the most-read page on the site. A fintech platform that has a strong certifications page — PCI DSS Level 1, SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001, local regulatory licences — and a public subprocessor list does not slow down in procurement. One that does not will spend weeks answering the same questions by email.

The security page does not need to be long. It needs to be specific, current, and linked to the actual certificates. A live uptime status page linked from the footer, showing 99.97% uptime over the trailing twelve months, does more for buyer confidence than any paragraph of copy.

HTML or WordPress — the Honest Comparison

Most fintech website templates on the market are built on WordPress with a page builder. They are flexible and they suit a team publishing compliance updates and blog posts every week. They also carry a running cost rarely mentioned at purchase: theme licence renewals, plugin subscriptions, security patches, and a developer on call the day an update breaks a payment-critical page.

A plain HTML fintech website template has none of that. No database means no database breach. No plugin stack means no plugin vulnerability. It loads faster, which matters for conversion on a paid acquisition funnel, and matters more for the signal it sends to a technical buyer evaluating a platform they are about to trust with their transactions.

The trade-off is real: editing content requires editing the file or asking whoever built it. For a payments platform whose product pages change a few times a year and whose compliance content is reviewed quarterly, HTML is cheaper and more defensible to own. For a team publishing three articles a week, WordPress is the better tool.

What to check before you buy any fintech website template

  • Does every page in the demo actually exist, or is it one layout repeated with different headings?
  • Does the pricing page have a real structure, or placeholder text and empty columns?
  • Is the compliance and tax section specific, or does it say "we handle tax" without naming a jurisdiction?
  • Does it hold together on mobile — the founder reading it on a phone between meetings?
  • Can you change the whole colour scheme without touching a hundred files?
  • What does it cost to maintain in year two — licence renewals, plugin updates, developer retainers?
