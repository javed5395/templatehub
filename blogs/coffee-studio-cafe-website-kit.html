Coffee Shop Website Template — A 9-Page Café Website Kit

LazyDogTemplates  |  LazyDog Studios
← Back to Journal

Search for a coffee shop website template and you will find hundreds of options — most of them variations on the same dark roast palette, the same overhead pour-over shot, the same "We are passionate about coffee" headline that every café in every city is somehow also using. Very few of them are built around what a coffee studio or independent café actually has to do online: convert a passing search into a first visit, turn a first visit into a regular, and make the website feel — without trying — like the place itself. This is a guide to what a café website needs to get right, and what to look for before you buy one.

What a Coffee Shop Website Is Really For

Most café visitors arrive at a website with one of three questions: is this place worth going to, what is on the menu and how much does it cost, and what are the hours and how do I find it. A website that buries the menu behind a PDF, hides the address in a footer, and leads with a slow-loading full-screen video of someone grinding beans has answered none of those questions and has cost itself visits it will never know it lost.

A café website has one job before all others: make it easy to decide to come in. That means the menu is a real HTML page the phone can load in two seconds, the hours are visible on the homepage, and the photography shows the actual space — not a set designed to look like your space.

Everything else — the brand story, the origin of the beans, the wholesale enquiry form — is secondary to those three answers. Design the hierarchy around that reality and the site works. Reverse it and the site is a piece of brand content that converts nobody.

The Nine Pages a Coffee Studio Website Needs

A café does not need twenty pages. It needs nine pages built well and kept current. A focused site with every page populated is far stronger than an ambitious one where half the pages say "coming soon" or redirect to the homepage. This is the set we build to:

  • 01 · Home — who you are, where you are, what makes the visit worth making. Hours and location in the first screen.
  • 02 · Menu — a real HTML page, not a PDF. Espresso, filter, food, seasonal specials. Updated when the menu changes.
  • 03 · Our Story — the founding, the sourcing philosophy, the people behind the bar. Written for someone who has never heard of you.
  • 04 · Gallery — the space, the bar, the latte art, the food, the light at different times of day. Real photographs of the actual place.
  • 05 · Events & Workshops — cupping sessions, barista training, private hire, live music evenings. The reason to come back beyond the coffee.
  • 06 · Beans & Wholesale — retail bags, subscription, wholesale for restaurants and offices. A separate page for a separate audience.
  • 07 · Catering — mobile bar, office catering, wedding service. The business-to-business offer, shown to the people who need it.
  • 08 · Reservations — table booking, private event enquiry, barista workshop registration. One form that handles all of them.
  • 09 · Contact & Location — address, map embed, phone, email, opening hours for each day of the week. The page that earns you the visit.

Nine pages work because they are navigable. The top menu stays simple — Menu, Events, Beans, Book — with catering, wholesale and the full story reachable from the footer and from contextual links within the pages. A visitor who can find what they need in two taps will come back. One who cannot will not.

The Menu Page Most Café Sites Get Wrong

The most common failure on a coffee shop website is the menu page, and it fails in one of two ways: it is a PDF that nobody can read on a phone without pinching and squinting, or it is an HTML page that was accurate the day the site launched and has not been updated since the oat milk surcharge came in.

A menu page on an HTML template should have a clean structure — espresso, filter, cold, food, seasonal — with each item on a line, the price beside it, and a brief note on the origin or preparation where it adds something. That is all. No background video, no parallax scroll, no hover animations on each line item. The visitor is hungry and they want to know if you have a flat white for under five pounds. Give them that answer first.

The seasonal specials section is worth its own heading, updated monthly. It signals that the menu is live and managed, which is a trust signal almost no café website bothers to earn.

Atmosphere Is Half the Product

In a café, the physical space is part of what is being sold. A customer choosing between two coffee shops at the same price and the same distance will choose the one whose website made them feel something — a sense of the light in the room, the pace of the morning, the kind of company they will be in. That is what the gallery page is for.

Three things make a café gallery page work:

  • Photograph the actual space. A set-dressed shot that looks nothing like the real café is a disappointment at the door. A photograph of a Tuesday morning crowd, slightly messy, entirely real, is an invitation.
  • Include people. Empty rooms look closed. A single barista mid-pour, a corner table occupied, a queue that is almost out the door — these signal that people choose to be here.
  • Update it seasonally. A gallery full of summer light in November tells a visitor the site is not being maintained, and a site that is not maintained raises a quiet doubt about whether anything else on it is current.

The Wholesale and Beans Page Nobody Thinks to Build

Most independent coffee studios that sell beans — retail bags, subscriptions, wholesale to restaurants and offices — fold that offer into the main navigation as a sub-item or mention it in the about section. Almost none of them give it a dedicated page.

A wholesale buyer from a restaurant group and a home espresso enthusiast ordering a 250g bag are the same person in terms of the transaction, and completely different people in terms of what they need from the page. The restaurant buyer needs minimum order volumes, lead time, price per kilo and a contact for a trade account. The home buyer needs to know the roast profile, the origin story, the grind options, and how long delivery takes.

A dedicated beans and wholesale page can speak to both without losing either. A page buried in the main navigation cannot. And because wholesale buyers search for phrases like "specialty coffee wholesale [city]" and "single origin beans supplier," a dedicated page with its own title and meta description earns search traffic that the homepage never will.

HTML or WordPress — the Honest Comparison

Most coffee shop website templates on the market are WordPress themes built on a page builder. They are flexible, and they suit a café with someone posting a new seasonal special every week or publishing monthly brewing guides. They also carry a running cost that is rarely mentioned at purchase: theme licence renewals, plugin subscriptions, and a developer on call the day a plugin update breaks the online ordering integration.

A plain HTML coffee shop website template has none of that. There is no database, no plugin stack, no login to be targeted. It loads faster, which matters for a visitor who found the café on a map and clicked through to check the hours while standing on the pavement. It costs nothing to maintain month to month if the menu does not change daily. And it can be handed to a web developer with a brief and a brand guide and built in a weekend.

The honest trade-off: editing the menu or the hours means editing an HTML file, or asking whoever built the site. For a café that updates its menu once a season and its events page once a month, HTML is cheaper to own. For a café with a team member publishing new content three times a week, WordPress is the better tool.

What to check before you buy any café website template

  • Does the menu page exist as a real HTML page, or is it a link to a PDF?
  • Does the gallery look like a real café, or a stock photography set?
  • Are the hours and address reachable from the homepage in one click?
  • Does it hold together on a phone — the most common device for someone deciding whether to visit right now?
  • Can you change the colour palette and fonts to match your actual brand without touching fifty files?
  • Is there a wholesale or beans page, or is that audience simply abandoned?
